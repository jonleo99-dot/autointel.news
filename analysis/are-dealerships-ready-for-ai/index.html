Are Automotive Dealerships Actually Ready for AI?

Artificial intelligence is spreading across automotive retail. But adopting AI tools and delivering an AI-ready buying experience are two very different things.

By Jonathan Leo•November 22, 2026•Research through November 21, 2026•~13 min read
In one view

Adoption is not readiness

Dealers increasingly use AI for customer follow-up, marketing and efficiency, but studies point to gaps in measurable returns, software handoffs, employee familiarity and readiness for AI-assisted shoppers. An operationally ready dealership must connect accurate inventory, clear transaction steps, responsible data handling and reliable human support—not simply accumulate tools.

The dealership has AI. Can it finish the job?

Imagine a customer shopping for a used SUV after the dealership has closed.

An AI assistant helps compare vehicles, narrow a budget and identify a potentially suitable listing. The shopper arrives at a dealership website with specific questions about availability, pricing and financing.

Then the experience starts to unravel.

The advertised price carries conditions that are difficult to interpret. The payment calculator opens another platform. The trade-in process requests information already entered elsewhere. A question requiring human assistance goes unanswered.

When the customer finally speaks with the dealership, the conversation starts over.

This is an illustrative scenario, not the result of an AutoIntel mystery-shopping investigation. But the underlying problems—repeated information entry, disconnected platforms and unsuccessful handoffs—are documented in automotive retail research.

The dealership might already use AI for lead follow-up, marketing, service scheduling or employee productivity.

Its AI tools may work. Its customer journey may not.

That distinction is central to determining whether dealerships are actually ready for AI.

The adoption numbers don't measure the same thing

Cox Automotive's AI in Auto Retail Tracker found that 82% of surveyed franchised and independent dealers reported using AI in some form during the second quarter of 2026. Common applications included task automation, customer follow-up and content generation. [1]

CDK Global's November 2026 AI at the Dealership study presents a different picture: nearly half of dealers actively using AI. [2]

Chase Auto's November Dealer AI Outlook adds a third perspective. Among nearly 200 surveyed dealership professionals, 80% reported some AI use, but only 54% described themselves as familiar or very familiar with the technology. [3]

These findings are not necessarily contradictory.

Cox's definition encompasses broad usage, including standalone applications and AI embedded in existing software. CDK emphasizes active use, while Chase examines both reported use and employee familiarity.

The studies also have different respondent populations, survey instruments and reporting methods.

Cox discloses its quarterly samples—483 dealers and 1,502 prospective vehicle buyers in Q2—and states that dealer responses were weighted by dealership size. Chase describes the characteristics and limitations of its nearly 200 respondents.

CDK's accessible November study summary does not provide enough methodological detail to establish a directly comparable adoption rate. Its findings therefore need to remain separately attributed.

The conclusion is not that one survey has identified the correct national percentage.

It is that using AI, understanding AI and achieving measurable business results are different stages of readiness.

That last distinction is particularly important.

In Cox's research, 69% of AI-using dealers expected the technology to generate sales and revenue growth, while only 22% reported experiencing that growth so far. Approximately one-third were not measuring AI's impact or lacked clarity about how they measured it. [1]

Those findings do not establish that AI has failed to deliver value. Employee productivity, better customer service and faster workflows may produce benefits that are not immediately reflected in revenue.

They do, however, reveal a problem with judging technology investments by adoption alone.

The customer is changing the starting line

AI-assisted vehicle research creates a challenge that extends beyond dealership-owned software.

Cox found that 63% of surveyed consumers intending to purchase a vehicle within 12 months said they definitely or probably would use AI during their next purchase. [1]

This measures stated intent, not completed AI-assisted purchases.

Nevertheless, the potential change in behavior is significant.

A buyer may use an AI assistant to compare models, estimate ownership costs, evaluate financing questions or identify suitable vehicles before contacting a dealership.

Chase's research adds evidence from the retail floor. Nearly half of respondents said shoppers were arriving better informed through AI research, although some also arrived with incorrect or outdated AI-generated information. [3]

This creates two responsibilities for dealers.

First, they must be prepared to answer more specific questions.

Second, they must be able to correct inaccurate information without losing the customer's confidence.

The dealership still needs to verify the actual vehicle, explain the offer, clarify financing assumptions and help the customer progress.

AI may change where the shopping process begins.

It does not eliminate the dealership's responsibility for what happens next.

The overlooked problem: systems that don't communicate

Many of the weaknesses affecting AI readiness existed before today's generative AI products arrived.

CDK's 2026 Friction Points Study found that 72% of surveyed dealership finance and insurance offices used four or more software systems to complete a transaction. [4]

Multiple specialized systems are not inherently a problem.

But when those platforms do not transfer information reliably, customers and employees encounter avoidable friction.

CDK also reported that one in four surveyed customers had to reenter information at the dealership after beginning the purchasing process online. [4]

Consider what happens when AI is added to that journey.

An assistant may respond immediately, identify a suitable vehicle and collect useful details. Yet if the next system cannot retain that information, the customer still experiences a breakdown.

Automation has made the beginning faster without fixing the problem farther along.

AutoIntel's September monthly analysis identified this same operating issue across service scheduling and F&I workflows: customers experience one dealership even when employees work through multiple separate platforms. [5]

That observation remains central to AI readiness.

The value of a technology should be judged partly by what happens after its own task is complete.

Does the next employee receive the relevant information? Does the appointment get confirmed? Can the transaction continue without duplicating previous work?

A connected journey matters more than a collection of individually capable tools.

What one dealership operator is doing differently

There are useful examples of dealerships treating AI as an operational problem rather than a technology purchase.

In a September 2026 interview with CBT News, Scott Falcone, owner of World Automotive Group, described developing AI applications around specific dealership needs. [6]

One example involved helping service advisors and technicians explain vehicle problems to customers in more accessible language.

Rather than beginning with a broad ambition to deploy AI everywhere, Falcone described identifying problems and building tools to address them.

He also distinguished between applications his organization could develop internally and larger systems—including CRM and compliance platforms—better left to specialized providers.

Falcone reported improvements across several sales and service activities. Those results are his account in a published interview, not independently audited findings or outcomes verified by AutoIntel.

The example nevertheless illustrates a meaningful alternative to indiscriminate adoption.

Start with the dealership problem.

Understand the people and systems involved.

Then determine whether AI is an appropriate solution.

It also raises a question that dealership leaders should ask before approving another vendor contract:

Are we solving an operational bottleneck, or simply adding another piece of technology?

The human handoff remains part of AI performance

AI assistants can handle many routine interactions efficiently.

But automotive retail is full of situations that require judgment, clarification or personal assistance.

A service customer may have multiple concerns. A buyer may need an explanation of financing conditions. Someone may need to correct inaccurate information supplied by an automated system.

Pied Piper's September 2026 Service Scheduling Effectiveness study evaluated 6,538 service requests across 33 U.S. automotive brands and four independent service-center brands. [7]

The study documented improvements in scheduling performance, particularly through dealership websites.

It also identified a persistent weakness: approximately one-third of attempted AI-to-human transfers failed.

Some customers reached voicemail, experienced disconnected calls or remained on hold without reaching the appropriate person.

That figure applies to attempted transfers in the studied service interactions—not all AI calls, all dealerships or vehicle sales.

Even with that limitation, the operating lesson is clear.

A successful first response does not guarantee a successful customer interaction.

Dealerships should evaluate whether automated systems recognize their limits, transfer customers appropriately and preserve the context needed to resolve the original request.

Speed is valuable.

Resolution is what the customer ultimately needs.

Can AI find and understand the dealership?

Another weakness emerges before a shopper ever visits the dealership website.

Cox found that only 29% of surveyed dealers were actively adjusting to AI-powered search. Another 32% recognized a need to adapt but had not started. [1]

Dealerships have historically invested heavily in their websites, inventory feeds, search optimization and lead-generation strategies.

AI-assisted discovery introduces additional questions about how readily outside systems can find and interpret that information.

Is the inventory current? Are vehicle specifications clear? Can shoppers understand pricing conditions? Are location and business details consistent?

These are not entirely new challenges.

Google's published guidance states that the established foundations of search optimization remain relevant to AI Overviews and AI Mode. It does not require special AI-specific structured data or promise visibility to websites that follow its recommendations. [8]

That guidance applies to Google's products, not necessarily to every AI assistant or retrieval platform.

For dealers, the practical objective should be reliable public information and measurable discovery performance—not promises of guaranteed AI recommendations.

The five dimensions of AI readiness

The available evidence suggests dealership readiness should be evaluated across five distinct dimensions.

1. Discoverability. Can shoppers find accurate, current information about the dealership, its inventory and its services through the channels they actually use?

2. Transaction readiness. Can customers move beyond browsing to meaningful next steps, including inquiries, appointments, financing exploration and trade-in information?

3. Continuity. Do customer details, questions and transaction changes survive the handoff between automated tools, departments and employees?

4. Trust and governance. Does the dealership understand how AI systems use information, who can access it, which vendors are responsible and how employees should handle errors?

5. Measurement. Can management connect a technology investment to a defined result, such as appointment completion, reduced reentry, improved response quality or more efficient operations?

These are AutoIntel's proposed evaluation dimensions, not a nationally validated scoring model.

They should also remain separate.

A dealership might have excellent internal systems but poor public inventory information. Another might have a polished website but unreliable handoffs between departments.

Both have readiness gaps, but the appropriate solutions differ.

A practical diagnostic: Follow the midnight buyer

One useful starting point is a documented walk-through of the public-facing buying journey.

Select an actual vehicle listing and attempt to answer five questions:

  1. Can the shopper identify the exact vehicle and understand its advertised details?
  2. Are pricing conditions and payment assumptions clearly explained?
  3. Can the shopper take a meaningful next step without unnecessary obstacles?
  4. Is there a clear route to human assistance when the automated experience reaches its limit?
  5. What evidence exists that the inquiry will retain its context as the journey progresses?

Record what is confirmed, what is incomplete and what cannot be verified from public information.

This exercise should not be mistaken for a complete dealership audit. CRM integration, actual staff follow-through, data security and transaction outcomes require additional evidence and, where appropriate, authorized access.

But even a limited test can identify where a customer's digital journey deserves closer attention.

Trust is not an optional feature

Dealership AI readiness also requires responsible handling of sensitive information.

For most dealerships involved in arranging consumer financing or leases, the Federal Trade Commission's Safeguards Rule requires a comprehensive information-security program covering relevant customer information. It also addresses oversight of service providers with access to that information. [9]

The precise requirements depend on the dealership's activities and the information involved.

As AI capabilities become embedded in dealership systems, managers need to understand vendor permissions, data retention, employee practices, error correction and appropriate escalation procedures.

More connections between platforms may create useful capabilities.

They can also create additional responsibilities.

A dealership should understand those responsibilities before making sensitive information available to another system.

Why readiness may begin with better management

Cox Automotive's 2026 Dealer Success Study offers a useful perspective on technology decisions.

Among franchised dealers in that research, 36% of those classified as strongly data-driven reported meeting profitability goals, compared with 19% of other dealers. [10]

These findings describe a survey association, not proof that data-driven management or AI causes improved profitability.

The study also found that data-driven dealers were more likely to consider whether a new technology product would improve data quality before purchasing it.

This connects directly to the AI readiness question.

A dealership that cannot identify its current bottlenecks may struggle to evaluate whether a new tool improves them.

A dealership that does not measure the results of its existing technology may have difficulty establishing the value of additional investments.

In some cases, the most effective next step may be employee training, cleaner inventory information or better integration between systems.

AI can make those processes more efficient.

It cannot automatically make an undefined or poorly managed process effective.

Where AutoIntel's assessment fits

AutoIntel.News has developed a Dealer AI Buyer Readiness assessment examining three public-facing dimensions: AI discoverability, transaction readiness and buyer friction. [11]

The approach is intended to document observable website capabilities and identify buyer-journey stages that need further investigation.

Its limits are important.

A public website scan cannot independently establish how employees use AI, whether private systems exchange information correctly, whether customer data is handled appropriately or whether technology investments generate a financial return.

The assessment is an AutoIntel-developed service, not independent evidence of nationwide dealership readiness.

Its value is in examining a defined part of the problem—not claiming to measure the entire business.

What to Watch Next

The next phase of automotive AI adoption should be measured less by product announcements and more by what customers and employees actually accomplish.

For dealership owners and general managers, the priority is identifying where important workflows fail and documenting whether changes improve them.

For technology providers, the opportunity is to demonstrate reliable integration, transparent limitations, appropriate safeguards and meaningful results.

For researchers, four questions deserve closer attention:

  • How accurately can AI-powered discovery tools identify real dealership inventory and pricing?
  • How much customer information survives the transition from AI-assisted research into dealership systems?
  • How often do automated conversations result in completed appointments, resolved inquiries or transactions?
  • Which operational practices distinguish dealerships achieving measurable AI value from those still struggling to evaluate their investments?

Answering those questions will require more consistent definitions, documented testing and additional direct dealer evidence.

Automotive dealerships are increasingly using AI. Whether they are actually ready for AI-enabled retail remains a different question.

The most prepared dealership may not be the one with the largest collection of AI tools.

It may be the one where the vehicle information is correct, the next step is understandable, the systems communicate and the customer never has to start the conversation over.
